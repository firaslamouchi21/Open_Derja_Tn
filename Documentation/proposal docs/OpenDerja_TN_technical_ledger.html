01

The proposal

What this is, what it isn't, and why I'm doing it anyway.

A platform that keeps running, openly licensed, where Tunisians write, correct and confirm their own regional Derja. Out of it comes a written corpus and a lexicon — balanced by region, layered by granularity, tied to real attestations, and available to download, browse, or query.

1.1What it is

  • Text first, voice by design. The platform runs on text from day one and carries voice attestation from a future update — not bolted on later. The consent model, the schema, and the tables for it (§7.11) exist from the first migration, before a single recording is collected.
  • Regionally balanced on purpose. Coverage per region is a number I track, publish and recruit against — not something I hope works out.
  • Alive, not archived. Academic corpora stop the day the paper ships. This one keeps growing.
  • CODA-compliant. Uses CODA-TUN and NOTA conventions instead of inventing another spelling standard.
  • Usable, not just downloadable. The corpus is exposed as a tool people have a reason to open, not only a file researchers have a reason to cite.

1.2What it isn't

Saying this out loud isn't modesty. It's what stops a researcher who knows the field from closing the tab in the first thirty seconds.

  • Not the first standardisation of Tunisian Arabic. CODA-TUN (LREC 2014) got there; NOTA replaced it.
  • Not the first Tunisian corpus. Tunisiya, TARIC, TSAC, TEC, TunSwitch, TEDxTN all exist.
  • Not the first Arabic dialect crowdsourcing platform. MyVoice/QCRI, DARIJA-C, KALAM'DZ came first.
  • Not competing with institutions that have funding and staff.
So what's left

No existing platform combines all of this in one place: text now and voice by design later; community confirmation instead of one authority's word; region and era tagged together, so old Derja sits next to today's four regions instead of being a separate archive; a working translator, not just a downloadable file; open data anyone can pull; and a shared place to publish and discuss the research itself. Every one of those pieces exists somewhere on its own. Nowhere do they exist together, continuously maintained, as one open service. That's the whole argument.

1.3What counts as success

LevelWhat it looks like
MinimumA published, citable, openly licensed, regionally tagged written Derja dataset that didn't exist before, with honest coverage stats — and a public tool built on it that a non-technical Tunisian can use without reading anything first.
GoodPeople contributing from all four regions without me pushing. People arriving through the translator and staying. Other researchers using the data. A working relationship with an established Arabic-NLP group.
StrongCited in Tunisian NLP work. A regional classifier and translation-memory service people actually use. Enough backing that maintenance isn't just me, forever.

1.4Why I'm building it

This is a non-profit effort, by design, from the first line of code. No company, no equity, no plan to ever charge for access, nothing here built to be sold. A public-good language resource doesn't get a paywall.

Twelve million people write this language every day — in texts, in comments, in the middle of French sentences — and there is almost no proper record of it. My grandmother says a word for something in the northwest that I have never heard anywhere else, and when she stops saying it, it's gone. Nobody wrote it down. That happens constantly, and nobody is on it.

"Somebody should write this down" has been true for decades. I'd rather be the person who did some of it. Even the minimum version leaves behind a dataset that didn't exist and a tool people can use — that's a real thing to have made, and it stands on its own even if nothing after phase 2 ever happens.

The other reason is just as real: it's a serious piece of engineering in a domain I care about, and I want to have built it. Both reasons hold at once.

The phases are scoped so the value survives even if the later ones never happen. If I run out of steam, that produces something finished, not something abandoned halfway.

Infrastructure and cost

Not decided yet, on purpose — and a much easier decision without a model on the box.

Where hosting stands

Hosting is still undecided. This section will be updated once it's decided. What this turns out to be determines what it needs to run on, so the specs stay open until the build is further along. Deployment is a phase 2 problem (§20). Nothing in phases 1–3 is blocked on it.

2.1Hosting

Provider undecided — to be updated here once it's picked. No permanent physical location, so self-hosted hardware isn't an option in any version of this; the decision is between a handful of small European or ARM-friendly VPS providers, sized against measured usage once the build is further along rather than guessed now.

2.2Deployment shape

VPS  (provider TBD — same topology regardless of which one)
 ├── cloudflared     tunnel — no inbound ports open on the box at all (§3.1)
 ├── caddy           TLS, reverse proxy, rate limiting, hostname routing
 ├── frontend        Next.js (public site + dashboards)
 ├── backend         NestJS — API boundary, guard chain (§10.1)
 ├── worker          background jobs, capped concurrency (§10.3)
 ├── postgres        single instance — the whole asset
 ├── pgbouncer       connection pooling in front of postgres (§10.2)
 ├── redis           cache + sessions only, not the job queue (§10.3)
 ├── nlp             Python, health-check only, phase 1 (§11)
 ├── processing      Rust, health-check only, phase 1 (§7.11)
 └── backups         nightly pg_dump, encrypted, -> off-box object storage

Ten containers instead of four — the backend split into its own process, pgBouncer and Redis added, and the two future-phase services showing up early as empty shells. All still multi-arch, so ARM against x86 isn't a decision I have to make in advance. Explicit memory limits stay on every one of them, which matters more now than it did at four services, not less.

The two health-check-only services and Redis are genuinely light — none of them hold real data or do real work yet, so the memory jump from adding them is small next to what a live ML model would cost (§11). pgBouncer itself is cheap; what it protects against is Postgres running out of connections, not memory. The number that actually matters is still whatever the VPS ends up being (§2.1) — this shape fits comfortably inside the small tier, it just isn't the four-box diagram it used to be.

2.3Backups

Domain name isn't picked yet — that gets settled once the build is done and I know what's actually running.

Backups, which are not negotiable

The corpus is the only irreplaceable thing here. I can rewrite the code in a weekend. I cannot rewrite months of community annotation.

  • Nightly pg_dump, compressed and encrypted before it leaves the box — the dump carries emails, ip_hash values and session ids, exactly what §4.3 works hard to be careful about, so it can't travel in the clear.
  • Weekly snapshot export to Hugging Face — a public release and an off-site backup in one
  • Provider snapshots on (a couple of euro a month, obviously worth it)
  • Restore tested quarterly — a scheduled verify-backup-restore.yml workflow (§10.0) runs it automatically, not a reminder on my calendar I can snooze. An untested backup isn't a backup.
  • Whole stack in docker-compose.yml, so moving providers takes under an hour

2.4Cost

Not worked out yet. There's no point pricing a box before I know what's actually going to run on it — cost gets figured out at launch, once hosting and the rest of this shape are actually decided, not guessed at now.

2.5Object storage — also undecided

Backups and snapshot exports need somewhere that isn't the application server, and that's undecided alongside hosting. Today's requirement is small — nightly dumps, weekly exports, comfortably inside anyone's free tier. The requirement if voice happens (§7.11) is a different question entirely, and it's the one that should decide this rather than the current one.

The axis that matters for audio isn't storage price — cheap everywhere. It's egress, because serving clips back costs real money and providers differ by an order of magnitude on it. Deciding this on today's backup needs would mean deciding it on the wrong criterion.

CandidatePosition
Cloudflare R2Zero egress fees. The default candidate the moment voice is on the table.
Backblaze B2Cheap storage, egress costs something. Fine for backups.
Provider-native storageCheapest if it ships from the same provider as the VPS anyway, not always S3-compatible the same way.
Plain S3Works, most expensive on egress, no reason to pick it first.

Decided in phase 2 alongside hosting, sized for backups, revisited before any audio work starts.

2.6Email

Reviewer invites and email verification need reliable delivery, and self-hosted mail from a fresh VPS IP lands in spam reliably — this bites hard on deploy day if it isn't planned for. Resend is primary, with SMTP as fallback rather than a second provider to configure (§10.0, §9.11): roughly €0–10/month at this volume.

AWS / AzureKubernetesmanaged PostgresCDNmulti-regionGPU instancesalways-on inference

If I want cloud provider experience for certification, that's a separate free-tier sandbox with throwaway resources. It doesn't go on this project's uptime and bill.

3.2Baseline

Rate limiting at Caddy, per IP anonymous and per key for the API. All input validated by shared zod schemas at the boundary. Parameterised queries only — Prisma handles it, no raw string interpolation anywhere. CSP headers, no inline scripts. Secrets in the environment, never committed, rotated on any suspicion. Admin subdomain IP-restricted where I can manage it. Dependencies pinned, advisory scanning on.

04

Legal, licensing, consent

Two licences, picked separately, and one checkbox you can't add later.

4.1Two licences

AssetLicenceReasoning
CodeMIT or Apache-2.0Permissive. Adoption matters more than control.
DatasetCC-BY-SA or CC-BY-NC — still decidingNot about protecting revenue — there isn't any. It's about stopping someone else from taking what contributors built for free, walling it off, and selling it back to them. Share-alike or non-commercial both close that door; plain-permissive doesn't. Needs a real decision, not a default.

Code credit lives separately from corpus credit. docs/CONTRIBUTORS.md names people who work on the codebase; the public /contributors leaderboard (§16.1) credits people who work on the corpus. Different contribution, different audience — folding the two together would bury the much larger group doing the actual data work under a handful of commit authors.

4.2Consent

An explicit checkbox at submission: this material can go into an openly licensed dataset, including commercial use. Two lines of UI. Without it no contributed material is ever safely usable commercially, and you can't collect consent later once contributors have moved on.

That consent covers the record as a whole, not just the raw text — including a reviewer later attributing an origin to a contributor's word, or tagging it vulgar, archaic, or regional. Those are judgments made by someone else, published alongside the contribution, and "this material can go into an openly licensed dataset" is written to mean the annotated record, not the bare string someone typed. Worth being explicit about rather than leaving as an assumption.

The voice clause ships with the first contribution, years early

The checkbox carries a separate voice clause from day one, even though recording doesn't exist and won't for a long time (§7.11). Text consent does not cover voice, and it can't be collected retroactively — the same argument twice as hard, because a recording identifies a person in a way a sentence doesn't. Default is text-only. Voice is a second, explicit opt-in.

4.3Revocation — the one thing that does hard-delete

Two rules in this document collide here

§17.2 says nothing hard-deletes — soft delete plus an audit entry, because the dataset is the whole asset. But hosting in the EU puts this under GDPR. If someone revokes consent, keeping their ip_hash and email around is a problem a soft-delete flag doesn't fix. A row marked deleted is still a row kept.

The way out is separating two things that were being treated as one: the person and the language. Personal data can genuinely be erased. The linguistic contribution isn't personal data once it's no longer attached to anyone.

on consent revocation (hourly job: anonymise_revoked)
  HARD scrub, irreversible, no audit copy kept:
    submission_meta.contributor_email, contributor_name, ip_hash, session_id -> null
    users.email, users.display_name -> null (if they had an account)
    audit_log.ip_hash -> null for that actor;  audit_log.diff scrubbed
  KEPT, permanently orphaned:
    the corpus item's text, its tags/tokens/links, its translations, its region/era/register,
    its dataset_split
  RECORDED:
    documents.rights_status -> 'revoked'; an audit entry with NO identifier — action, timestamp, count
  • The text stays, the person goes. Published snapshots aren't affected — they never carried identifiers (§19.2).
  • Irreversible and that's the point: admin-only, typed confirmation, rate-limited.
  • Full removal is honoured on request; revocation defaults to anonymisation.

Voice is the exception to the exception. When recordings exist (§7.11), revoking voice consent deletes the object and the media row outright — it can't fall back to anonymisation the way text does, because a recording is biometric-adjacent. That's the second and last place anything is genuinely destroyed, designed in now before any recording exists.

4.4Scraped material

Stored with its source and licence flag, kept out of the commercially clean tier by default. Public isn't a licence — scraped text is research-use unless there's evidence otherwise (§12.2). Removal requests honoured through the flag mechanism (§16.4) and source quarantine. No personal data from social sources — author identifiers stripped at ingest.

4.5Crediting prior work

CODA-TUN, NOTA and any corpus I reuse get cited on the methodology page and in the dataset card.

Four regions, six axes

Get this wrong and everything downstream is wrong with it.

6.1Regions — flat, no subregions

ValueCovers
northwestKef, Jendouba, Siliana, Kasserine, the western highlands
northTunis, Bizerte, Nabeul, Ariana, Manouba, Ben Arous
sahelSousse, Monastir, Mahdia, Sfax, the coastal centre
southGabès, Medenine, Tataouine, Tozeur, Kebili, Douz, Gafsa

Four values, no subregions until there's data to justify them. The subregion structure exists from day one and sits empty — adding it later is a migration on one column, not a schema change.

6.2Six independent axes

This is the decision everything hangs off. Region, era and setting aren't the same kind of thing and must never be squashed into one list.

AxisValuesNote
scopepan_tunisian · regionalWhether it's marked at all, not how widely it's used
regiona set of: northwest · north · sahel · southOne or more. Empty when pan-Tunisian. §6.4.
subregionempty (reserved)Deferred
eracontemporary · historical"Old Derja" lives here, not in region
settingurban · rural · unknownCuts across region, doesn't follow it
registerneutral · formal · vulgar · archaicWithout it the output isn't usable for much. Display policy in §16.4.

"Northwest + rural + historical" is a real thing you can ask for. Four flat buckets couldn't say it — and that combination is exactly the material nobody has collected, which is most of the reason this exists.

6.3Granularity — another separate axis

Pan-TunisianRegional
wordshared vocabularya northwest word for an everyday object
sentencephrasing used everywherea construction that marks where you're from
paragraphneutral connected textconnected speech with regional marking

6.4Region is a set, because dialect is a continuum

Isoglosses don't respect buckets. A rural word can be shared between the northwest and the south and be unknown in Tunis. Coastal vocabulary can cover the north and the sahel and stop there. Neither is pan-Tunisian, and neither belongs to one region.

With a single-value column every case like that has two bad options: pick one region and throw away a real attestation, or call it pan-Tunisian and throw away the fact that it's marked at all. Both wreck the coverage numbers, which are the thing this project claims to be good at. So:

  • scope is markedness. Does this carry a regional signal at all? That really is binary.
  • region is extent. Which regions attest it, one to four. All four isn't the same claim as pan-Tunisian — "used everywhere and marked in each" differs from "unmarked".

So there's no single-value region column anywhere. Corpus items carry several tag rows on the same span (§7.3); lexicon variants use a lexicon_variant_regions table (§7.5), which also gives each region its own attestation count.

This also lets the region-boundary question (§5, problem 3) answer itself. If northwest and south keep getting tagged together, that's evidence about where the boundary actually falls. A single-value column would have destroyed that evidence at insert time, every time.

07

Data model

One structure with span-level annotation, because the levels bleed into each other.

7.1The joining problem

A sentence is often marked as regional by one word in it. A paragraph can quote a variety it isn't written in. Three independent tables can't hold that, so it's one structure with annotations attached to character spans.

7.2Documents and corpus items

documents
  id, source_id fk -> sources
  external_ref      text          -- video id, url, isbn; null for contributions
  title, raw_body   text          -- raw_body is the original, never touched again
  region_hint       enum          -- weak signal from the source, not a fact; single-valued on purpose
  era_hint, license enum
  rights_status     enum          -- granted | revoked | not_applicable (§4.3)
  ingested_at       timestamptz

corpus_items
  id, document_id fk, parent_id fk    -- parent null at top level
  unit              enum      -- paragraph | sentence | phrase      (no token)
  position          int       -- order within parent
  text              text      -- immutable once inserted
  script            enum      -- arabic | arabizi | mixed | latin
  canonical_form    text      -- CODA form, null until standardised
  canonical_map     jsonb     -- offset alignment: text -> canonical_form
  match_key         text      -- normalised for search, never shown (§13.5)
  rule_version      int
  char_offset       int       -- offset in parent, for reconstruction
  dataset_split     enum      -- train | dev | test; set at insert, never changed
  version           int       -- bumped on any accepted change (§8.1)

  index (document_id, unit, position)
  index (parent_id)
  index gin (match_key gin_trgm_ops)

Contributions get wrapped in a synthetic one-corpus-item document pointing at the contribution source. That's deliberate rather than tidy: license, rights_status and provenance live on documents and nowhere else, so "can this be exported commercially?" is one lookup against one table whatever the origin. A nullable document_id would mean copying those columns onto corpus items and splitting that check in two forever.

region_hint stays a single enum even though §6.4 spent real effort making region a set everywhere else. That's deliberate, not an oversight: a hint is a guess about where a whole source probably comes from — a channel, a forum's general userbase — before anyone has read a word of it. It's allowed to be wrong and singular because nothing downstream trusts it; the actual region claim, which does need to be a set, lives on the tags attached to spans (§7.3), not here.

Why tokens aren't rows here

The obvious move is a third level under sentences. It breaks the moment you standardise anything. Converting to CODA changes word count and every offset after the first edit — Arabizi "bch nemchi" becomes باش نمشي, clitics expand, two tokens turn into three. Every token row and every annotation pointing at one quietly stops lining up. Nothing errors. The data just starts lying.

So tokens are their own table (tokens), anchored to offsets in text. text never changes, so those offsets stay right forever. A separate links table is the one bridge from a token to the lexicon — token_id is unique there, so nothing stops one token pointing at two different lexicon entries, a constraint a single shared table couldn't express at all.

Standardisation never edits text. It writes a second version alongside, plus canonical_map — the offset alignment the rule engine produces anyway (§13.4). Any span on the original projects onto the canonical form through that map, and since the map carries its rule_version, changing rules regenerates both together.

7.3Tokens, links, and tags — on spans, not levels

This is what sorts out the contamination. A paragraph tagged south can hold a three-word span tagged as a northern borrowing. Both true, both stored, no argument.

Three tables, not one polymorphic annotations table — the single-table version is where the naming fog crept in, because a type: region row and a type: token row have nothing in common except sharing a table. Splitting them out turned out to be a real bug fix, not just cosmetic: it's what let links.token_id be declared unique, below.

tokens                        -- a span inside one corpus item's text
  id, corpus_item_id fk
  char_start, char_end   int
  surface_text   text     -- the literal token text
  annotator_id fk    -- the 'system' user for machine-run tokenisation
  is_machine   bool
  confidence   numeric(3,2)
  index (corpus_item_id)

links                         -- the ONE bridge from a token to the lexicon
  id, token_id fk UNIQUE      -- one link per token, never two
  lexicon_entry_id fk, lexicon_variant_id fk null
  annotator_id fk, is_machine bool, confidence numeric(3,2)
  index (lexicon_entry_id)
  index (lexicon_variant_id)

tags                          -- everything else: scope, region, era, setting,
                               -- register, code_switch, sense, quality
  id, corpus_item_id fk
  char_start, char_end   int
  kind    enum   -- scope | region | era | setting | register
                 -- | code_switch | sense | quality
  value   text
  annotator_id fk    -- the 'system' user for machine suggestions
  is_machine   bool
  confidence   numeric(3,2)

  index (corpus_item_id, kind, char_start, char_end)
  index (annotator_id)

Tokenising is its own pass, inserting one tokens row per token — re-tokenising means inserting rows, not rebuilding a tree. A region tag is one row per region — tagging a span northwest and south is two rows, which is how §6.4's set semantics work without an array column.

Three things this gives free

Code-switching — a French loanword is a span with kind: code_switch, value: fr. Corpus and lexicon join up — a links row pointing a token at a lexicon entry turns "this sentence attests the northwest form" into one query. Disagreement survives — two annotators write two tags rows; consensus is worked out from them, never written over them.

7.4Consensus — derived, never destructive

Keyed by span, not by corpus item, and that matters more than it sounds. One sentence routinely carries several tags of the same kind — three code_switch spans, two spans with different regional identities. (Links don't need this — one token has at most one link, so there's nothing to reach agreement over.) Key it by corpus item and kind alone and computing consensus for the second span overwrites the first. The more carefully someone tags a sentence, the more of their work the table throws away.

tag_consensus                -- materialised, refreshed when tags are written
  corpus_item_id fk, kind enum, char_start int, char_end int
  agreed_values       text[]         -- a set: region can agree on more than one
  agreement           numeric(3,2)   -- 1.0 = unanimous
  annotator_n         int
  needs_adjudication  bool
  computed_at         timestamptz

  PRIMARY KEY (corpus_item_id, kind, char_start, char_end)
  index (needs_adjudication) where needs_adjudication
  • Whole-item tags still get a span. register, era, scope are written with char_start = 0, char_end = length(text). Never null — Postgres doesn't treat nulls as equal in a unique key, so a nullable span would let duplicates through in exactly the place uniqueness is the point.
  • Agreement runs over a set. Two people who both say "northwest and south" agree. One saying "northwest" and one saying "northwest and south" partly agree — the truthful reading, and a useful signal for the boundary question.

7.5Lexicon — three layers

lexicon_entries               -- the meaning, region-agnostic
  id, gloss_en, gloss_fr, gloss_msa
  domain   enum   -- everyday | food | admin | agriculture | kinship | ...
  granularity enum, pos enum, notes

lexicon_variants               -- one realisation of a lexicon entry
  id, lexicon_entry_id fk
  scope, era, setting, register     -- single-valued axes only
  canonical_form  text
  match_key       text              -- §13.5
  confidence      numeric(3,2)

lexicon_variant_regions        -- extent: one row per attesting region (§6.4)

7.6Word origin

Derja's layering is its history — Amazigh substrate, Arabic superstrate, then French, Italian, Turkish and Spanish arriving through occupation, trade and migration. Recording which layer a word comes from turns a wordlist into a document of that history, and it's the most interesting thing on the site to anyone who isn't a linguist. No existing Tunisian corpus carries systematic etymology tagging.

It hangs off lexicon_entries, not lexicon_variants — origin is a property of the word's history, not of how Kef says it versus Douz. The exception is real but rare: sometimes regions borrowed different words for the same meaning from different sources. That's arguably two lexicon entries, and I'd rather let the rare case carry a note than duplicate the column onto variants for a minority of entries.

7.7Translations — explicit keys, not polymorphic

One phrase can have several valid translations, so translations are rows. But how they point at their target matters as much as that they exist.

The tempting shape is target_type plus a bare target_id uuid. Postgres can't constrain that at all: no foreign key, no delete behaviour, no guarantee the id points at anything real. Delete a variant and its translations become orphans nothing in the database can catch. They pile up quietly and eventually ship inside an export.

translations

    (corpus_item_id IS NOT NULL)::int + (lexicon_entry_id IS NOT NULL)::int
    + (lexicon_variant_id IS NOT NULL)::int = 1 )

  index (lexicon_entry_id, target_lang) where lexicon_entry_id is not null
  index (lexicon_variant_id, target_lang) where lexicon_variant_id is not null

  unique (corpus_item_id, target_lang) where is_preferred and corpus_item_id is not null

The one place I keep polymorphism, on purpose. corrections (§7.8) still uses target_table + target_id, because a correction can target nearly any field of nearly any table and three nullable columns would become fifteen. Accepted trade: it can't cascade and gets validated in app code. Survivable because corrections are a workflow record, not corpus content, and because nothing hard-deletes (§4.3).

Basic means basic. Phase 5 voice is attestation audio: someone records a clip of a variant they already submitted, and it plays back on the lexicon entry. No ASR, no TTS, no forced alignment, no speech models. The clip proves a written form really is said that way, by someone from there — the one thing text can't prove on its own.

Three things audio changes structurally, which is why it isn't just "a migration later":

  1. Storage leaves Postgres. A corpus item is ~1KB. A ten-second clip is ~150. That's object storage with a lifecycle policy, and pg_dump doesn't cover it — a second backup path with its own economics and restore test (§2.5). The stored_objects row is resolved through a presigned URL — the clip itself never passes through the API. The provider behind it is pluggable (local disk in dev, R2 or S3 in production, one interface, §10.0) — built as control-plane/backend/core/src/storage/, with both a local and an R2 adapter already written, just not wired into a route yet — so this decision doesn't get re-made twice.
  2. speakers has no text equivalent. A recording attests a variant as spoken by a person whose origin may not match the variant's region.
  3. Consent stops being scrubbable. §4.3 works because a sentence with identifiers stripped is genuinely anonymous. A recording isn't — it's biometric-adjacent and you can't anonymise it by nulling a column. Voice revocation means deletion, the second exception to "nothing hard-deletes".
08

The task engine

The piece I'd most regret getting wrong.

Everything below is about curating and verifying material that already exists in the pipeline — reviewing, tagging, translating, adjudicating. It's not the only way in. Anyone can add brand-new material directly, no task required: a new word, sentence or paragraph at /contribute, a new word origin at /origins, a new citation at /research. This section is the review layer sitting on top of that, not a gate in front of it.

Contributing isn't only writing something and walking away, either. Whoever writes a corpus item can optionally translate it themselves, add an Arabic or Arabizi rendering, and say which region it belongs to — standard pan-Tunisian Derja, or a specific region if they know it. Anything they skip doesn't get lost or left half-finished: it becomes exactly the same translate_*, transliterate_* or region_tag task (§8.2) that a scraped corpus item would have spawned. Writing your own material and filling in someone else's are the same task queue, just entered from opposite ends.

One status column can't do this

An entry can be translated but not transliterated, or transliterated but not reviewed. Separate axes of done. A single status column forces them into an order that doesn't exist and makes half-finished work invisible.

8.1Schema

tasks
  id, corpus_item_id fk, type enum
  status    enum   -- open | claimed | done | rejected | needs_rework
  priority  int
  claimed_by fk null, claimed_at, completed_by fk null, completed_at
  output_record_id uuid     -- points at the row it produced
  requires_role  enum
  target_regions enum[] null   -- the regions in conflict, or the hint (§8.5)
  item_version int
  idempotency_slot text not null default ''
  idem_key  text   -- sha256(corpus_item_id : type : item_version : idempotency_slot)

  index (status, type, priority desc)
  index (claimed_by, status)
  index (corpus_item_id)

  -- at most ONE live task per (corpus item, type, version, slot)
  unique (idem_key) where status in ('open','claimed','needs_rework')
Why the idempotency key exists

"Open task Y if it isn't already there" sounds fine until someone corrects an already-processed corpus item. The correction lands, the corpus item changes, the spawn rules fire again and re-queue translate_msa and link_lemma — both already finished. Refine another annotation, same thing. The queue fills with duplicates of closed work and reviewers stop trusting their inbox, which is the one surface the whole contribution loop runs through.

  • corpus_items.version goes up on any accepted change — a correction applied, a standardisation, an adjudicated tag. It's what "this task is about this state" means.
  • idem_key is sha256(corpus_item_id : type : item_version : idempotency_slot). Same four inputs, same key, duplicate rejected by the index instead of queued.
  • idempotency_slot separates several legitimate tasks of the same type on one corpus item. Empty for item-scoped work (review, region_tag, translate_*, transliterate_*, standardise), char_start:char_end for span-scoped work (link_lemma, adjudicate), an ordinal for the N-of-a-kind case (confirm → 1, 2). Leave it out and the unique index quietly collapses two confirmations into one, one lemma link per sentence instead of one per token, and a second disputed span into nothing. None of which raises an error.
  • The index is partial, over live statuses only. Putting status inside the unique key instead looks equivalent and isn't — it constrains historical rows too and blocks legitimate rework transitions. Limiting it to open/claimed/needs_rework says the actual intent: never two live tasks for the same work, leave the completed history alone.
  • A version bump is what properly reopens work, and not every bump invalidates everything. Each rule declares which changes kill its output — a region re-tag doesn't invalidate a finished transliteration; a standardisation does invalidate link_lemma. That mapping sits in the rules module as data.

Built. Schema, atomic claim, complete/reject/rework lifecycle, the idempotency key exactly as above, and the spawn rules for every row in §8.3 — all live in control-plane/backend/core/src/tasks/, a framework-free package the NestJS layer calls into. review, region_tag and confirm completions are wired end to end and have been exercised against a running database. translate_*, transliterate_*, standardise, adjudicate and link_lemma completions aren't wired yet — they need the lexicon and translate modules, which are still empty stubs, and link_lemma additionally needs a tokenisation pass that doesn't exist yet, since nothing currently writes tokens rows.

8.4Claiming and release

  • Claiming is atomic — UPDATE … WHERE status='open' RETURNING. Two workers never get the same row.
  • Claims expire; a job releases anything held over two hours.
  • One worker holds at most 5 claims at once, so nobody hoards the queue.
  • needs_rework isn't rejected: rework goes back in the queue, rejection closes it.

8.5Regional routing, and the bias it prevents

The failure I'm designing against

When consensus drops below threshold an adjudicate task spawns. If the reviewer pool is northern-heavy — and early on it will be, because that's where my network is — a northern reviewer can confidently reject a perfectly good southern variant just because they've never heard it. That doesn't lose a row. It systematically strips out the exact material this project exists to collect, while looking like quality control the whole time.

So adjudication doesn't go to whoever's free. The task carries target_regions — a set, because §6.4 made region a set and a northwest-versus-south conflict has two regions in it.

on spawn adjudicate:
  target_regions = the conflicting tag regions, or the corpus item's document's region_hint

  1. offer ONLY to reviewers whose self-reported region is IN target_regions
  2. after 7 days unclaimed -> open to everyone, flag out_of_region, and say so:
     "no reviewer from this region was available"
  3. out_of_region adjudication can confirm or escalate to admin.
     Never reject on regional grounds, never delete a variant.

A reviewer matching any region in the set counts as in-region. The seven-day window is per task, not per region — otherwise a two-region conflict waits twice as long as a one-region one, which is backwards, since disputed cases are the ones that most need deciding.

  • Same idea for region_tag and confirm: a confirmation from a reviewer of that region counts at full weight, one from outside counts for less.
  • Out-of-region rate is on the admin overview (§17.1). If it climbs, the answer is recruiting in that region — problem 4 in §5 — not relaxing the threshold.
  • This is why region_self_reported is on the users table and gets asked at reviewer invitation, rather than being optional profile decoration.

8.6What this makes easy to answer

What's unfinished by type and age. Which region has the most stalled work. Who's contributing what and how fast. Whether the pipeline is jammed at review, translation or standardisation. All queries, not investigations.

Accounts and permissions

Friction kills volume. Anonymity kills quality. Pick per role, not once.

9.1The tension

Every step between "I want to help" and "I submitted something" costs contributions, and contributions are the resource this whole thing runs on. But annotation quality needs accountability. One policy can't serve both, so there isn't one.

Why contributors don't get real accounts

Somewhere there's a version of this where contributors sign up properly — email, password, verification, a profile. It's cleaner. Every rule gets easier. I'd know exactly who wrote what.

It's also a fantasy, because nobody is doing that. Picture it: someone taps a link, they're curious, they've got maybe fifteen seconds of goodwill, and the first thing they meet is Create your account. Gone. Not annoyed, not weighing it up — gone, instantly, the way I'd be gone. I'd be asking a stranger to open an account on a website they've never heard of, run by a guy they've never heard of, so they can donate a word for free.

So contributors get a cookie and a name box, and I live with weaker identity guarantees. That's the whole trade, and it isn't close.

9.6The hierarchy is internal

Trust levels, roles, task types, spawn rules, consensus thresholds, adjudication routing — all of that is how the system stays honest. None of it is a contributor's problem and none of it appears in front of them.

A contributor sees one box, one question, a button. They don't see their trust level, don't know that finishing a region tag spawned two confirmation tasks, don't pick a task type, and are never told they're ineligible for anything. Work they can't do yet just isn't offered — no locked state, no progress bar toward a permission, no explanation of a system they didn't ask about. Promotion happens silently: more kinds of work start showing up.

The leaderboard is the only visible status anywhere, and it's a ranking rather than a rank — who has contributed, not what tier anyone sits in.

The rule for every contributor-facing screen

One question, three seconds, no explanation required. If a screen needs a paragraph of instructions, the screen is wrong, not the person reading it.

Every sentence between arriving and submitting comes out of a budget measured in seconds. I don't have a citation for how many and I don't need one — I know how fast I close a tab that wants me to read something before it'll let me do anything, and I've got no reason to think anyone is more patient with mine than I am with theirs.

All that complexity stays fully instrumented. It just faces the admin dashboard (§17.1) instead of the contributor.

9.7Token and session design

JOSE (HS256), not a random JWT library — one well-audited implementation rather than picking whichever package has the most stars this year. A token carries four things beyond the usual sub and expiry: token_version (bumped to invalidate every token for a user at once — the "log out everywhere" and "something's wrong, kill this account's sessions" button in one field), sid (session id, so one login can be revoked without touching the others), rjti (a rotating token id on the refresh token specifically, so a stolen refresh token that gets used twice is detectable — reuse means someone else has it). Passwords are argon2id, reviewer- and admin-only since contributors don't have passwords at all (§9.3).

9.8Session flow

Short-lived access token, longer-lived refresh token, standard pair. Refresh rotates the token on every use — old rjti stops working the moment a new one is issued, which is what makes reuse detection possible. Logout revokes the sid server-side rather than just deleting a cookie client-side, so a stolen token doesn't stay valid after the legitimate user thinks they've logged out. Bumping token_version is the blunt instrument for "everything, everywhere, right now" — used for a password change or a suspected compromise, not for routine logout.

9.9Email verification and password reset

Both run through a single-use, time-limited OTP sent by email, not a magic link with a long-lived token sitting in someone's inbox indefinitely. Rate-limited per address and per IP, and the response is identical whether or not the address exists in the system — "if an account exists, we've sent a code" — so the endpoint can't be used to check who has an account here.

9.10Reviewer and admin login specifics

  • Failed logins lock the account temporarily after a handful of attempts, with backoff, not a hard permanent lock a legitimate user then has to email me about.
  • TOTP 2FA is mandatory for admin and superadmin, optional but encouraged for reviewers.
  • GitHub OAuth is preferred over password for reviewers specifically because most of them are likely to be technical people who already have a GitHub account — one less password for me to be responsible for.
Deliberately not ported from anywhere else

A few patterns that show up in other systems I've built or seen and don't belong here, said explicitly so nobody wonders why they're missing: impersonation (an admin logging in "as" a user) — nothing here needs it and it's a liability sitting unused. National ID / QID encryption — this project never collects a government identifier, so there's nothing to encrypt. Payments — covered already (§4), there is no paid tier, so there's no payment flow to secure. A separate mobile token flow — there's no native app (§20), so there's no separate client to design tokens around.

9.11Email delivery

Outbox pattern: writing "send this email" is a database row in the same transaction as whatever triggered it, not a direct call to an email API in the middle of a request. A worker job dispatches the outbox asynchronously and retries on failure. That means a flaky email provider can never fail the actual action — a signup succeeds even if the verification email is delayed a few minutes, instead of the whole request failing because Resend had a bad second. Resend is primary, with SMTP as a fallback path rather than a second thing to configure and forget about (§2.6).

10.1Shape

Two apps, not one, and still one repo. NestJS owns the API boundary and the guard chain — auth, validation, business logic, data access. Next.js owns rendering — the public site and the dashboards — and talks to NestJS only through a typed client, never straight to the database. The file tree is §10.0; nothing below re-derives it.

Splitting the app didn't change why it's one repo. Enums — region, task type, role, script, origin — are defined once in shared and imported by both sides, so a dropdown and a database constraint can't drift apart because they're literally the same file. Zod schemas validate at the boundary and type the forms from one definition. One install, one CI pipeline, one deploy — that part of the reasoning didn't need to change just because the backend got its own process.

Not microservices, and since machine assistance is deferred (§11), not doing any real ML work either — the two service containers in §10.0 exist to prove the deployment shape, not to make decisions. When batch inference eventually happens it runs off-box (§11.3), so it never becomes a third real workload on this box.

10.2pgBouncer

Sits between the app and Postgres in transaction-pooling mode, pool size 20. Postgres has a hard ceiling on concurrent connections, and NestJS, the worker, and pg-boss all opening their own connections directly is exactly how a small box runs out of them under real load — not a hypothetical, a known failure mode for anyone running Postgres behind more than one process.

One invariant worth stating plainly so it doesn't get rediscovered the hard way: DATABASE_URL points at pgBouncer and is what the app uses at runtime. DIRECT_URL points straight at Postgres and is what Prisma migrations use — transaction-mode pooling doesn't support the prepared statements and session-level features migrations need. Two URLs, two jobs, and mixing them up is the kind of mistake that only shows up once, at the worst time.

Redis earns its place for cache and sessions (§9.7), not for the job queue — those are different jobs, and giving them to the same system would mean Redis going down takes both out at once. pg-boss stays on Postgres for the queue, for the same reason it was chosen before Redis was ever in the picture: one less service to run, one less thing to back up, and the volume here never gets near justifying a second queue technology doing the same work Redis could theoretically also do.

Machine assistance — deferred

Planned, scoped, and not built from day one.

The decision

There's no machine learning in this system at launch — no model, no inference, nothing in the request path that can be slow, wrong, or down. There is a Python service from phase 1, deliberately minimal: a health-checked FastAPI container with no model loaded and no meaningful memory footprint, existing solely so the deployment shape — a container, reachable, health-checked, sitting in the compose file — is proven early instead of showing up as a phase-4 surprise. The distinction that matters is unchanged: the service exists, but nothing in it makes a decision yet. Dialect classification, embeddings and machine translation are still phase 4 — and when they show up they run off-box, not on the application server.

11.1Why it's deferred

  • It was the biggest thing driving infrastructure cost. A MARBERT-class model sits on 1.5–2GB. Even loading it only at night, firing it up while Postgres is chewing through the queue is a realistic way to get OOM-killed. Dropping it doesn't manage that risk, it deletes it (§2).
  • It doesn't do the interesting part. There's no pretrained model at Tunisian sub-region level. A classifier can tell you a sentence is Tunisian. It can't tell you it's from Kef and not Sfax. That call — the actual contribution here — is human either way.
  • You can't train it before the corpus exists. Regional classification needs this dataset first. Building the consumer before the input is backwards.

11.2What does the job instead

Rules: script detection, length bounds, a regex marker pre-filter, near-duplicate removal (§12.3). Weaker, and §12.3 says exactly what that costs. I'll take it over needing a real model and a GPU on day one — the sidecar already exists (§10.0), it just isn't doing anything yet. Transliteration is unaffected — that was always a rule engine (§13).

11.3What phase 4 might look like

Not planned in any real detail — this is phase 4, and I'll design it properly when I get there. Just so it's not a total blank, roughly what I'd reach for:

  • A dialect pre-filter — something MARBERT-class, run offline over the backlog to cut reviewer load, not to make decisions.
  • A regional classifier, trained on this corpus once it's big enough — the actual research contribution, and the reason the corpus has to come first.
  • Hosted embeddings + pgvector for fuzzy matching. Exact and trigram lookup already ship in phase 2 with no model at all (§14.2), so this only covers the fuzzy case.

Whatever it ends up being, it runs off-box, billed by the second, not on the application server — that part I'm sure of, since it's what keeps the box small (§2). And whenever machine output does arrive it enters as a suggestion, is_machine: true, never straight into a canonical field (§7.9, §12.4). That rule doesn't wait on the details above being settled.

Rights policy for scraped text

Every source is checked against its terms of service, not just its robots.txt — the two are separate documents and permission from one doesn't imply the other. Scraped and stored text is treated as research-use only and kept out of the commercially clean export tier by default (§4.4), regardless of the source. Data protection follows the GDPR posture in §4, since hosting sits in the EU.

Why open dumps beat platform APIs, stated once so I don't forget it. Licence has to travel with the data. A CC-BY-SA dump downloaded once is mine to keep and republish indefinitely. Anything accessed through a platform API carries that platform's leash forever — refresh obligations, revocable authorisation, terms that can change under me with 30 days' notice. A corpus meant to outlive my attention can only be reliably built from the first kind, which is the real reason Wikipedia, Common Crawl and Tatoeba are in and YouTube, Reddit and Twitter/X are out — not just that the APIs cost money.

The filter logic that decides what a scraper keeps versus throws away isn't decided yet either — what's below is the shape of it, not a final ruleset. Expect the actual thresholds and markers to move once real data starts coming through.

Reviewer queue fatigue — what dropping the classifier costs

Rules alone let more noise through than a model would. Best estimate: dropping a machine-learning dialect filter raises noise leakage — MSA and other Maghrebi dialects that aren't actually Tunisian Derja — from somewhere around 20–30% up to 40–50% of what reaches the queue. That's a real, noticeably heavier manual review load through phases 1–3, not a rounding error.

The fix is throughput, not cleverness: small ingest batches, the marker list kept as versioned data so it improves from real rejections, and a source's reject rate treated as a reason to switch it off. Whether it's worth pulling a lightweight dialect filter earlier than phase 4 to cut that leakage sooner is a real question I haven't settled — noted here rather than decided.

Scraping doesn't serve the regional-signal argument — a real limit, not a footnote

A scraped sentence has no region metadata. None. So how does a reviewer tag it northwest? By recognising northwestern markers in the text — which means the regional label on scraped material encodes what reviewers already believe is regional, not independent evidence that it actually is. That's circular. Feed that into a classifier later (§11.3) and I've trained a model to reproduce my reviewers' priors and called it a finding.

Contributed material is different in kind: someone says "I'm from Kef, we say it like this." Weak evidence, but independent of the linguistic features I'm trying to learn — testimony, not inference. Consequence, as policy: scraped corpus items default to region: unknown rather than being tagged from linguistic intuition. A reviewer may tag a scraped item's region only when a contributor from that region has independently attested the same variant. Scraping buys volume and general Derja coverage. It cannot, on its own, buy regional claims — elicitation is the load-bearing pipeline for what this project is actually differentiated on.

12.4Dedup applies to contributions too

MinHash sits in the scraper pipeline, which leaves an obvious hole: someone submits a sentence already in the corpus, and it sails through as new work for a reviewer to reject.

So the same near-duplicate check runs on the contribution path, against match_key — and the response is the interesting part. Not "rejected, duplicate", which reads as a slap. Instead: we've already got this one — but nobody has recorded how the south says it. Do you know? That turns a rejection into an elicitation prompt, which is the same trick §14.4 plays on the translator's misses.

12.5Staying inside the rules, not just avoiding a ban

Two different problems live here and they need two different fixes. Getting IP-banned is an operational annoyance. Violating a platform's terms of service, or a data protection law, is a different kind of problem — the fix isn't a proxy, it's not scraping that source at all (§12.2).

  • Every source gets its ToS read before its first run, not just its robots.txt checked. The two are different documents answering different questions, and a site can allow crawlers in one while forbidding storage in the other.
  • One server means one IPv4, so scraping at volume from a single address risks a ban even on a fully permitted source. Budget for proxy rotation on anything HTML-parsed — planned for up front rather than discovered the week after the first ban.
  • Rate limits and backoff are per-source config, on the source row, editable from the admin panel, exponential backoff on 429 and 403.
  • Two failed runs disables the source and raises an alert (§17.1), instead of continuing to hammer a host that already said no.
  • Scrape slowly and constantly rather than in bursts. There's no deadline — this is supposed to accrue for years, and being polite is both the compliant choice and the operationally cheaper one.

12.6Rules

  • Raw material is immutable. Every stage reads one representation and writes a new one — which is exactly why a source with a mandatory refresh/delete clause (§12.1) can't be a source here.
  • Machine output — whenever it exists — comes in as a suggestion, never as fact.
  • Every source is registered with its default licence and its ToS status from §12.2, so rights attach at ingest instead of being guessed later.
  • Scrapers respect robots.txt, rate limits, and the site's terms of service — the first two are not a substitute for the third.
  • Author identifiers stripped at ingest. No personal data from social sources, ever (§4.4).

Standardisation, transliteration, matching

Three different jobs on the same text. Keeping them apart is the whole trick.

13.1Don't invent an orthography

CODA-TUN (LREC 2014) and NOTA already define conventional spelling for Tunisian Arabic, and CODA* maintains a verified spelling database across dialects. Using them beats inventing something: peer-reviewed, free, and it makes this dataset work with the whole CAMeL and MADAR toolchain. Inventing a rival standard costs months and makes the data less useful at the end of it.

13.2Rules are versioned data, not code

standardisation_rules
  id, rule_version, pattern, replacement, script, description, active

A spelling rule will change six months in. If canonical forms get overwritten in place with no version recorded, every snapshot you've already published becomes irreproducible and internally inconsistent. Rules as versioned rows make regeneration deterministic — and re-runnable at an older version, which is what makes a bad rule bump recoverable (§17.2).

13.4The standardisation workflow

  1. The rule engine produces a suggested canonical form and marks the spans it wasn't sure about.
  2. The reviewer sees the suggestion with those spans highlighted and fixes them inline.
  3. The accepted form is stored with its rule_version and with canonical_map — the offset alignment between original and canonical form, which the engine already computed while applying its rules. That map keeps token spans (§7.2) usable against both versions of the text.
  4. Corrections that keep recurring are candidate rules. The reviewer UI surfaces the manual overrides people make most often.

13.5The match key — a third representation

Postgres has no Arabic text search configuration

This matters and it's easy to miss. Postgres ships text search configs for English, French, Spanish, Turkish and a dozen more. There is no Arabic one. to_tsvector('arabic', …) doesn't exist. Any plan that says "we'll use full-text search" for the translator is writing a cheque the database won't cash.

14

The translator

The reason anyone opens the site. Ships in phase 2, needs no model.

14.1What it is

  • Derja → MSA / French / English. Any script, any region.
  • MSA / French / English → Derja, with a region selector. Nobody else offers this direction. "Say this in southern Derja" is something no existing tool can do, and it's the demo that makes people send the link on.

Output isn't one string. It's the attested forms with their regions, their scripts, a link to the corpus item each came from, and — where anyone recorded it — where the word came from (§7.6). That last detail costs nothing and does a lot of work: seeing that a word you use every day arrived through Ottoman Turkish is what makes the page feel like a reference work instead of a lookup table.

14.2It needs no model

  • Word and phrase — lookup through lexicon_entries → lexicon_variants → lexicon_forms. Exact match on match_key, then normalised match through the standardisation rules, then pg_trgm similarity for typos and spelling variation (§13.5).
  • Sentence — trigram similarity over corpus_items.match_key, returning attested corpus items with their human translations. Not full-text search, for the reason in §13.5.
  • Region filtering — a lexicon_variant_regions join. Already in the schema.

No embeddings, no API key, no cost, nothing in the request path that can be down.

14.3Why it improves on its own

The property that makes this the right shape

Every accepted annotation improves the translator the moment it's written. No retraining, no snapshot to promote, no regression risk. A trained model improves in versioned jumps and can get worse; a retrieval translator can only get better, and it gets better the same day someone contributes. That's worth more here than fluency — it makes the tool a live reason to contribute instead of a thing that consumes contributions.

The page says so, with a live counter: this translator knows N words and M sentences across four regions, up from X last month. On day one that counter says something embarrassingly small and I'm leaving it visible anyway, because "12 words, growing" is a more honest pitch than a splash page pretending to be finished.

14.4Failing honestly, and the loop that comes out of it

When there's no attestation, the translator doesn't guess. It shows which words it found and which it didn't, the nearest attested neighbours marked as approximate, what's missing by region — "attested in north and sahel. No southern attestation." — and a one-click contribute prompt for exactly that gap.

Every failed lookup is an elicitation prompt

The translator's gaps are the /gaps page, generated from real demand instead of guessed at. Someone from the south who searches for a word and sees "nobody has recorded how the south says this" is the best contribution prompt in the system, and it costs nothing to produce.

14.6What it isn't

The page says what it is: a corpus lookup, not machine translation. It returns what Tunisians have actually written, or it returns nothing. That's a weaker product than a fluent model and a more honest one, and the honesty is why a researcher can cite it.

The same caution covers anything generated. A model trained only on this corpus will be bad for a long time, and putting one in front of people early burns credibility with exactly the audience I need. Generation is phase 5, labelled, never mixed in with attested results. When models arrive they train against frozen snapshots and get promoted only on measured improvement against a held-out split that never enters training — no continuous retraining, because that gives no reproducibility, no way to spot a regression, and quietly absorbs bad annotations.

What this costs: the lexicon lands in phase 2

lexicon_entries, lexicon_variants, lexicon_variant_regions, lexicon_forms and lemma-linking are what the translator runs on, so they belong in phase 2 rather than with the rest of the service layer. Lemma linking is core annotation work here, not a late nicety.

16.4Two things public pages have to decide

Reporting

§4.4 promises removal requests get honoured quickly, which is hard to do with no way to make one. Every permalink gets a report link writing to flags (§7.8), feeding the corrections lane. I will get slurs, insults, someone's phone number, and material a person wants gone — that's not pessimism, it's what happens to every site that accepts public text. A button is cheaper than an inbox.

Vulgar register

register: vulgar exists because Derja is full of it and excluding it would falsify the corpus. But /explore and /translate are public and unauthenticated, so the policy has to be stated rather than left to whoever writes the query:

  • Filtered by default, with a visible opt-in toggle. The toggle is honest about what it does — it doesn't say "safe mode", it says "include vulgar register". The preference lives in a cookie, same idea as the session cookie in §9.3 — no account needed to keep the choice between visits.
  • The API carries the same switch, not a separate policy. /v1/search and /v1/lookup both take an include_vulgar parameter (§18.1) that does exactly what the UI toggle does. Without it, both default to filtered — the API doesn't get a quieter or looser rule than the page does.
  • Always present in exports, always labelled. A corpus that quietly drops half of how people actually speak isn't a corpus of how people speak.
  • Never filtered in the review queue. Reviewers see everything; that's the job.

17.2Sections

Data management

  • Browse every table with filters — a safe read-only view
  • Bulk operations: re-run standardisation on a selection, reassign region, bulk reject a bad scrape batch
  • Merge duplicate lexicon entries; split a lexicon entry that conflated two meanings
  • Every bulk operation writes an audit_log row with a reversible diff
  • Translator miss log — most-searched unattested terms, ranked, exportable as elicitation prompts

Review lanes

  • Community corrections, proposed word origins, submitted publications and flags all ride the one corrections lane rather than getting queues of their own — flags get a bare list view from phase 2 (§20), folded properly into this unified lane once the rest of it exists in phase 3
  • Publication comments awaiting approval, and the switch that turns commenting off entirely (§7.10)

Task administration

  • Reprioritise queues — push southern-region tasks up when balance slips
  • Force-release stale claims, reassign, manually spawn tasks for a selection
  • Pause a task type entirely — suspend translation while standardisation catches up
  • Adjudication routing: review out-of-region cases, adjust the 7-day window, see the rate per region
  • Queue throttle: poll interval, concurrency, batch size, live (§10.3)

Sources

  • Add, edit, disable; per-source default licence and consent status
  • Schedule, rate limit, proxy pool, yield history
  • Manual run; last error with stack trace
  • Quarantine a source and bulk-flag everything it produced, if the rights turn out murky
  • Source failures and the nlp/media health checks (§17.1's Service health row) alert through the same Sentry path — one notification mechanism, not two

Users and contributors

  • Promote to reviewer; adjust trust manually with a recorded reason
  • Per-contributor quality: approval rate, disagreement rate, regions they submit from
  • Ban by session or ip_hash; bulk-revert everything a bad actor submitted
  • Invite reviewers by email with expiring tokens, capturing their region

Standardisation rules

  • Full CRUD, with a preview showing how many corpus items a change would touch before applying it
  • Bump rule_version, then regenerate canonical forms and offset maps as a background job
  • Frequent-override report: the corrections reviewers keep making by hand — the candidate new rules
A bad rule bump needs a way back

Regenerating every canonical form from new rules is exactly the operation you discover was wrong afterwards. So: snapshot before regenerating, keep the prior rule_version rows active rather than deleting them, and make regeneration re-runnable at any earlier version. Rolling back is then re-running an old version, not restoring a backup at 3am.

Snapshots and releases

  • Create: freeze, checksum, count, release notes
  • Pick export tiers — full, commercially clean, etymology, per-region
  • Push to Hugging Face and object storage via presigned URLs, never through the NestJS process — same mechanism §7.11's media storage will need later
  • Diff two snapshots: corpus items added, changed, removed; rule version delta
  • Deprecate with a reason, shown publicly on the download page

Privacy operations

  • Process a consent revocation with typed confirmation (§4.3)
  • Preview which fields in which tables a revocation will scrub, before running it
  • Subject access request: export everything held about one session or account
  • Revocation log — action, timestamp, count, and no identifiers in it

System

  • Job status, failure log, manual retry
  • Backup status: last successful dump, size, destination, encryption confirmed, last restore test — populated by verify-backup-restore.yml (§2.3), not a calendar reminder to check manually
  • Feature flags — turn a page on for reviewers before it goes public
  • Maintenance mode with a public banner
  • Full audit log, searchable by actor, entity, action, date

Model management — phase 4 only

Register a model against a snapshot with its metrics; compare a candidate against production on the held-out split; promote or roll back. Empty until §11 happens — specified now so the frozen-split discipline is designed in rather than bolted on.

Rules for anything destructive

Nothing hard-deletes. Every destructive action is a soft delete plus an audit entry. Bulk operations over a threshold make you type the affected count to confirm. This dataset is the whole asset — the admin panel should make it hard to damage even when I'm tired and moving fast.

Phase 2 does not end with a dataset

The obvious phase 2 goal is "something citable exists" — a download page and a DOI. Right artifact for a researcher, wrong one for everybody else, and everybody else is who this needs. Every hard problem in §5 — reviewer recruitment, regional balance, staying alive — is a problem of getting people to show up and keep showing up. A dataset can't do that.
